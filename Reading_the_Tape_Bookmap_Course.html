Week One
Foundation — what you're actually looking at

Before patterns, before signals, before any decision logic, you need to understand what the Bookmap heatmap is showing you. Most traders open Bookmap, see swirling colors, and start hunting for setups. They never quite escape the feeling that they're guessing. The fix is simpler than it looks: spend the first week understanding the picture itself, and refuse to extract conclusions until the mechanics are second nature.

The order book in plain language

Every market has two stacks of orders sitting at the exchange: people who want to buy at specific prices below the current market (the bid) and people who want to sell at specific prices above the current market (the ask). These are limit orders — they wait passively until someone else comes along willing to take them.

Price moves only when an aggressive participant — someone using a market order — comes in and consumes one side of that book. A market buy lifts the offer; a market sell hits the bid. The book then updates: that price level is consumed, and price either moves to the next level (if the level was small) or holds (if the level was large enough to absorb the aggression).

That single mechanic — limit orders waiting, market orders eating them — is the entire game. Everything you're going to learn in this course is a variation on watching that interaction happen in real time.

What the Bookmap heatmap actually shows

The horizontal axis is time. The vertical axis is price. The colors show resting limit orders at each price level over time. Bright/intense color means lots of resting size sitting there. Faded color means little or none. As time advances and orders accumulate, get pulled, or get consumed, you see a moving picture of where size is building and where it's evaporating.

The single most important habit to build this week: understand that bright bands of color are not predictions. They are statements about what currently exists. When a bright band is consumed, that's an event — that's where information is created. When a bright band gets pulled before being touched, that's also information, often more useful than the consumption.

Mental model

The heatmap is a topographic map of intent. Bright zones are mountains of resting orders. Price is a ball rolling across the terrain. Your job over the course is to learn which mountains stop the ball, which ones get steamrolled, and — most useful of all — which ones disappear right before the ball arrives.

Volume bubbles and the price ladder

On top of the heatmap, Bookmap overlays volume bubbles — circles whose size represents the amount of aggressive trading at each price and time. A big circle means a lot of contracts crossed the spread there. Color shows direction: green for buy aggression (market buys lifting the offer), red for sell aggression (market sells hitting the bid).

This is the second half of the picture. The heatmap shows resting intent; the bubbles show aggressive action. The interplay between them — big bubbles eating bright zones, big bubbles failing to move price through bright zones, bright zones disappearing before bubbles arrive — is where every meaningful pattern in this course lives.

Week Two
Liquidity — where the big players hide, and when they're lying

Now that the picture makes sense, the question becomes: when a bright cluster appears on the heatmap, who put it there and what do they actually intend to do? Not all liquidity is real. Some of it is bait. Some of it is a placeholder. Some of it is a serious commitment that you can lean against. Learning to tell them apart is the work of week two.

The four kinds of resting size you'll see

Every bright zone on the heatmap is one of these. Learning to identify which is which, in real time, is roughly half of practical tape reading.

α
Stacked Liquidity
Real walls · large size sitting in commitment

A wide, persistent bright zone that has been sitting at a price level for many minutes or hours. It survives nearby price action. When price approaches, it doesn't pull. This is what real institutional intent looks like — somebody has decided that price is the level, and they're putting capital behind it.

On Bookmap, the visual signature is a thick bright band that maintains intensity over time. The longer it has been there, the more weight the market gives it.

β
Iceberg Orders
Hidden size · refresh patterns

A price level shows modest size on the book, gets traded into, and immediately reappears at the same level. Then again. And again. What you're watching is a much larger order being sliced — only a fraction is shown publicly at any moment, but each time aggression eats it, more is replenished from the hidden remainder.

On Bookmap, icebergs look like a level that absorbs more aggressive volume than its visible size could possibly account for. The heatmap shows a thin band at the level, but volume bubbles around it are huge and price doesn't move through. This is the highest-quality "real" liquidity signal in the entire course.

γ
Pulled Liquidity
Vanishing books · the most informative event

A bright cluster appears, sits for a while, and then — as price approaches — disappears. The orders were withdrawn before being tested. The placer didn't actually want to defend that level. They wanted the appearance of defense, or they wanted to influence other participants' decisions, or they got nervous.

This is more useful information than absorption or even initiation. When liquidity pulls in front of approaching price, the level is going to break. Hard. Watch for this constantly. The instant a bright zone goes dim as price closes in, the trade is to follow through, not fight it.

δ
Spoofing
Fake intent · illegal but persistent

A large order flashes onto the book away from price, sits briefly, then cancels — never intended to trade. The purpose is to influence other participants into believing there's resistance/support somewhere, so they trade ahead of it in the desired direction. The spoofer profits from the resulting move and never has to actually defend the level.

On Bookmap, spoofs look like brief bright flashes that appear and disappear without price ever testing them. Single instances are noise. Repeated patterns at similar levels — appear, sit 10-30 seconds, vanish — are spoofs and you should treat that level as meaningless.

Reading the difference in real time

The fastest mental shortcut: time on the book is conviction. A cluster that has sat for 30 minutes is far more meaningful than one that appeared 30 seconds ago. When you scan the heatmap and see brightness, your first question should be "how long has that been there?" — and Bookmap's history view answers that directly.

The second question is "how does it react when price gets close?" Real liquidity sits and absorbs. Fake liquidity pulls. You don't need to predict which it is in advance — you just need to watch what happens when the test occurs.

GC FocusWhere gold's real liquidity lives

Three locations matter most for GC. Memorize them; they recur every session.

  • Round number handles ($10/oz boundaries — 4500, 4510, 4520, etc.): Real participants stack at these levels routinely. The deeper the handle (4500 vs 4505), the more size tends to gather there. When GC is approaching a round handle, scan the heatmap for stacking that has built up over the last hour. That's the level the next move will fight or break.
  • Prior session high and low: The visible H/L of yesterday's regular trading hours acts as magnet liquidity. Stops sit just beyond. Limit orders sit just before. The interaction at these levels is where many of GC's cleanest moves originate.
  • Asia session range extremes: Particularly relevant given your existing system. The Asia high and low become reference points London and NY will test. Liquidity stacks at these prices throughout the night.
The single most important week-2 takeaway

Pulled liquidity is the most actionable single pattern in the entire course. When a bright cluster goes dim as price approaches, the next 10-20 ticks tend to come fast in the direction of the prior move. This is the one pattern you should be hunting for in every session you watch this week.

Week Three
Aggression — who's pressing, and how hard

Resting orders set the stage. Market orders write the story. Week three shifts attention from the heatmap to the volume bubbles — the actual aggression crossing the spread, the one thing that genuinely moves price. By the end of this week you should be able to look at any minute of GC tape and answer two questions instantly: who is pressing, and is anyone home to stop them.

What the volume bubbles really say

Each bubble represents the contracts that traded aggressively at a given price during a given moment. Green bubbles = market buys lifting the offer. Red bubbles = market sells hitting the bid. Size of the bubble = number of contracts. The position of the bubble vertically tells you the price; horizontally tells you the time.

What this means in practice: every time you see a green bubble, somebody decided they needed to be long badly enough to pay the spread. Every red bubble is the same thing in reverse. The size and frequency of those decisions tell you whether participation is growing or shrinking, and which side has the conviction.

Cumulative Volume Delta — the running scoreboard

CVD is the running sum of (buy aggression − sell aggression) over time. When CVD rises, more contracts are being aggressively bought than aggressively sold. When CVD falls, the reverse. The line itself doesn't predict price — but the relationship between CVD and price tells you whether a move is real or a head-fake.

Price action CVD action Interpretation
RisingRisingHealthy uptrend — buyers really are buying
RisingFlat or fallingDivergence. Price is rising on lack of selling, not real buying. Fragile.
FallingFallingHealthy downtrend — sellers really are selling
FallingFlat or risingDivergence. Price is falling on lack of buying, not real selling. Bounce candidate.
Flat / rangeStrongly trendingAbsorption — one side pressing, other side passively eating it. Range will eventually break in CVD direction.

Speed of tape

Volume bubbles in rapid succession at large size = aggressive participation. Sparse, small bubbles = quiet. The rate at which bubbles appear gives you a feel for whether you're in a moment of real flow or just drift.

Some traders watch this in time-and-sales windows; on Bookmap, the visual rhythm of bubbles arriving works just as well and is faster to read peripherally while you're watching the heatmap proper. By end of week three you should be able to glance at any 30-second window of tape and say "fast" or "slow" without thinking about it.

GC FocusWhen gold actually has flow

Gold is not constantly liquid. The aggressive flow comes in bursts, mostly tied to:

  • Asia open (17:00 CT) and the early evening: Modest flow as Asian institutions wake up. Your existing 7:50-8:10 PM CT window sits in this zone.
  • London open (02:00 CT): First real wave of European participation. Flow doubles or triples within minutes of the open.
  • NY open and the first 90 minutes (07:30-09:00 CT): The deepest, fastest tape of the day. Most aggressive moves originate here.
  • News releases: Fed, CPI, NFP, geopolitical headlines. Flow goes from sparse to violent in seconds. Don't trade through these while learning; do watch them in replay.

Outside these windows, GC drifts. The bubbles are sparse, the heatmap is mostly stable, and it's hard to learn from the tape because not much is happening on the tape. Spend your screen time during the active windows.

The key shift

Until now you've been watching where price is and where it might be drawn to. From this week on, you also watch how it's getting there. A 10-tick move on big aggressive bubbles with rising CVD is a different animal from a 10-tick drift on no volume at all. Same chart pattern; opposite implications.

Week Four
Absorption — when aggression fails, the highest-EV pattern in tape reading

If there is a single pattern worth all six weeks of work, it is absorption. Every other pattern in tape reading is a variation, an antecedent, or a derivative of this one event: aggressive flow arrives, hits a price level, and stalls. The aggression is real — bubbles are big, the tape is fast — but the price doesn't move. Somebody is sitting there, eating everything that arrives, and refusing to budge. When you see this, you are watching a fight in real time, and one side has already decided how it ends.

What absorption actually is, mechanically

Imagine 500 contracts of aggressive market buying hitting an offer. In a typical move, those 500 contracts would push price up several ticks as they consumed level after level. In absorption, the 500 contracts hit the offer and price doesn't move. That means somebody on the other side had at least 500 contracts of resting limit sell orders sitting at that price, and they were willing to fill the entire aggression without giving ground.

Why does this matter? Because when one side throws everything it has at a level and the level holds, the aggressive side has exhausted itself. The next move is in the opposite direction, because the side that absorbed was the side with conviction and capital. The aggressive side just spent its ammunition and got nothing.

What absorption looks like on Bookmap

The visual signature is unmistakable once you've seen it a few times:

  1. Price stalls at a level — three, five, ten bars trading in a tight band
  2. Volume bubbles are large at that band — heavy aggression keeps arriving
  3. Heatmap stays bright at the absorbing level — orders refresh as fast as they're consumed (an iceberg pattern from week 2)
  4. CVD continues to trend in the aggressive direction even though price isn't moving — the divergence pattern from week 3
  5. Eventually, the aggression peters out — bubble sizes shrink, tape slows — and price reverses sharply

That fifth point is when the trade exists. The reversal off absorption is fast and clean because the absorbing side has been winning the entire fight; once aggression dies, there's nothing stopping the other direction.

The mental script

When you see price stalled at a level with big bubbles arriving, ask: "Is this aggression making progress?" If volume is huge but price isn't moving, the answer is no. Aggression that doesn't make progress is exhausted aggression. Trade the reversal, not the continuation.

Absorption vs accumulation — a critical distinction

These look similar but mean different things:

  • Absorption happens at extremes — a high being tested, a low being probed, a level being broken. The aggression is concentrated, fast, and gets defeated by a wall. The reversal is sharp.
  • Accumulation happens over longer time frames — hours, sometimes days. Persistent two-sided activity in a range with a slight directional bias. Price moves laterally while one side quietly builds a position. The eventual breakout is in the accumulation direction.

Both are useful. For 20-tick scalping, absorption is the higher-leverage pattern because it resolves in minutes, not hours.

Failed breakouts as absorption tells

One of the cleanest expressions of absorption: a breakout setup that fails immediately. Price pushes through a prior high on volume, can't extend, and gets violently sold. What you watched was absorption at the breakout level — limit sellers were waiting above, ate every aggressive buy that arrived, and then some larger seller (or a flush of stops) drove the reversal.

For your existing candle system, this is directly relevant: the 9.4% of your setups that fail catastrophically are very often absorption-against-trend events. The 3-bar momentum that triggered your entry was the aggressive side. The catastrophic loss came when that aggression hit absorption. Knowing how to spot the absorption forming would identify those failures in advance.

GC FocusWhere gold's absorption is highest quality

  • Prior day H/L tests: The single highest-EV absorption pattern on GC. When price approaches yesterday's high or low on aggression and stalls there, the absorption is institutional and the reversal is reliable. This is the trade idea you should be watching for in every NY morning session for the rest of your career.
  • Round-handle defenses: $10 boundaries on GC. When price gets pushed into a $10 level on heavy bubbles and stalls with a bright heatmap defending, the level usually holds and the bounce is 15-30 ticks.
  • Asia high/low at NY morning: When NY tries to break through Asia's range and gets absorbed, the reversal back into range is a high-probability scalp.
A discipline note

Absorption is the most psychologically difficult pattern to trade because you're going against the apparent momentum. Every visual cue you've trained on for years says "this is going up — don't sell here." The tape is telling you the opposite. This is precisely why most retail traders never learn it: it requires trusting tape over price action when the two disagree. You're not trying to do this yet. You're learning to recognize it.

Week Five
Initiation — when real moves begin

Absorption is what stops moves. Initiation is what starts them. The two are mirror images of each other, and together they account for the great majority of meaningful price movement. By the end of week five you should be able to distinguish between drift, false starts, and genuine initiation — and that distinction is precisely what your candle system needs in order to filter the 9.4% failures from the 90.6% wins.

What initiation actually is

Initiation is the opposite mechanic of absorption. Aggressive flow arrives at a level. The book gives way. Price moves through the level into thinner liquidity, and the move accelerates because each successive level fails to defend itself. Whereas absorption is "aggression met by an immovable wall," initiation is "aggression met by an empty room."

The key visual signature: price moves cleanly through resting liquidity that should have stopped it. The heatmap shows what looks like meaningful resting orders ahead, and then those orders get vaporized — either consumed or pulled — and price rolls forward.

The four-part initiation sequence

Real initiation almost always unfolds in this sequence. Recognizing it as it forms is the work of the week.

  1. Setup: Price compresses near a key level. The book builds on both sides. Tape is moderate. CVD is neutral or mildly trending.
  2. The first push: A burst of aggression in one direction. Several large bubbles in quick succession. Price moves a few ticks. CVD spikes.
  3. The book reaction: This is the moment that defines whether initiation is real. Either the resting liquidity stays put and absorbs (false start, fade trade), or the resting liquidity pulls and price rolls through (real initiation, follow trade).
  4. The follow-through: If real, the next 10-30 seconds bring continued aggression in the same direction, with price moving cleanly through additional levels. This is when the 20-tick move materializes.

Pay particular attention to step 3. The decision to take or skip a candle entry should hinge here. If you see price push, then see resting liquidity ahead pull or evaporate, you're watching real initiation. If you see price push and the liquidity ahead stays put or grows, you're watching a setup for absorption.

Failed initiation — the fade signal

Sometimes initiation looks real for the first 5-10 seconds and then dies. Aggression spikes, price moves a few ticks, and then... nothing. The bubbles get smaller, the tape slows, the heatmap rebuilds in the direction of the move.

This is your "false start" pattern. Real initiation builds momentum bar over bar. Failed initiation peaks and reverses. When you see a candle setup form on the back of failed initiation, that's a low-quality entry — the same trigger that would have been A-grade with real initiation behind it. By week 6 this distinction will be visible to you in real time.

Combining initiation with your candle system

Your 3-candle trigger is, mechanically, a momentum-continuation setup. It works when the momentum is real and fails when the momentum is fake. Initiation tells you which is which. The mental decision tree at the moment of trigger:

Tape evidenceAction
Real initiation in trigger direction (book gave way, CVD aligned, fast tape)A-grade — take normal size
Drifting move with no clear initiation, CVD neutralB-grade — smaller size or skip
Failed initiation visible, or absorption forming against the moveSkip — this is where the 9.4% lives
Counter-trend trigger against absorbing wallSkip the trigger, watch for the reversal as a separate trade idea

By the end of the week you should be able to look at the tape during this window and tell the difference between drift, false starts, and initiation within 30 seconds of activity beginning.

Bridge to your system

Initiation is the single concept most directly applicable to your candle scalping. The 3-candle pattern fires equally well behind real initiation and fake momentum — but the outcome of those two cases is wildly different. Learn to see initiation forming, and you've identified your discretionary filter.

Week Six
Integration — your filter, defined and disciplined

Five weeks of pattern exposure. Five weeks of watching, journaling, and matching tape to outcome without trading off the tape. The work of week six is consolidation: turning what you've absorbed into a written, repeatable filter that sits between your candle trigger and your entry click. By the end of this week, you should have a personal decision protocol you can articulate in two sentences and execute in under five seconds.

The decision tree

Here's the structure to start with. Treat it as a draft your live experience will refine over the next 30 days. The point is not that this exact tree is correct for you — it's that you have a tree at all, in writing, that you can audit and improve.

Pre-trade checklist · 5 seconds at the moment of trigger
  1. Session check. Am I in an active GC window (Asia, London, NY morning)? If no — skip.
  2. Initiation check. In the 60 seconds preceding this trigger, did I see real initiation in my direction (book gave way, aggressive bubbles, CVD aligned)? If no — skip or take half size.
  3. Absorption check. Is there a visible absorption pattern forming against my direction at any nearby level? If yes — skip.
  4. Pulled-liquidity check. Did liquidity in front of price recently get pulled in my direction? If yes — A-grade, take full size with confidence.
  5. Default action. If all checks are clean and at least one is positive: take normal size. If checks are mixed: take half size or skip per your discretion.

Position sizing by tape grade

One of the most important lessons of this course doesn't relate to entry — it relates to size. Once you can grade tape quality, you can scale size accordingly. This is far more powerful than just refusing bad trades, because most setups aren't binary good/bad — they're shades of gray.

Grade Tape quality Position size
A Real initiation + pulled liquidity ahead + aligned CVD Full normal size
B Aligned tape but no clear initiation; or initiation with mild divergence Half size
C Drifting move, neutral CVD, no obvious tape support Quarter size or skip
F Absorption against your direction, or visible failed initiation Skip — no exceptions

Your existing system makes money at indiscriminate sizing because the candle pattern is positively skewed. Adding even rough tape grading to size decisions should push your expectancy meaningfully higher without changing what you trade — only how much you trade it.

Journaling the integration

For 30 days after this course ends, every trade you take goes into a structured journal. The columns are simple but rigorous: timestamp, direction, candle setup quality, tape grade (A/B/C/F), size taken, outcome (target hit / stopped / managed), final P&L in ticks, and one sentence on what the tape was doing at entry.

After 30 days, sort the journal by tape grade and look at win rate and expectancy in each bucket. This is your evidence. If A-grade trades win at 95% and F-grade you skipped trades have an obvious different profile, you have your filter and you trade it. If the buckets show no separation, you haven't learned the filter yet and you keep watching.

What stays the same

Your candle system. Your existing risk management. The instruments you trade. The accounts you trade them in. The position-sizing-to-withstand-drawdown approach that has been working. Nothing about your existing edge changes. The only thing being added is a 5-second filter step between trigger and entry.

What changes is the trades you skip. After a few weeks of disciplined application, you should be skipping 30-50% of triggers. The trades you take should be cleaner, more often work quickly, and the average drawdown on winners should drop meaningfully. The catastrophic 9.4% should diminish — not vanish, but materially shrink — because the absorption-failure pattern that drives those losses is exactly what week 4 trained you to spot in advance.

The trap to avoid

After this course you'll be tempted to start taking trades off the tape itself — reading absorption and trying to fade, reading initiation and trying to chase. Don't. The course was designed to give you a filter for your existing system, not a new system. Tape-only entries require an additional level of skill and a different risk framework. That's a separate project, for next year if at all. For now: candle trigger + tape filter + your existing rules. Nothing more.

Appendix A

Pattern Flashcards

Quick reference for the eight patterns covered. Print this section if you'd like a desk-side reference. Each card includes the visual signature, what it means, and how to act on it within your existing candle framework.

α
Stacked Liquidity
Real walls · large persistent resting size
Visual
Thick bright band sitting at one price for many minutes or hours, surviving nearby price action.
Meaning
Institutional commitment to a price level. Real defense.
Action
Treat as a magnet that price will test, and a wall that price will struggle to break. Trades in the direction of approach often fade off the wall.
β
Iceberg Orders
Hidden refresh size at a price
Visual
Modest visible size at a level, but volume bubbles arriving at that level are repeatedly larger than the visible size could account for, and price doesn't move through.
Meaning
A large order being sliced and refreshed. Highest-quality real liquidity signal.
Action
Skip candle triggers that point into an iceberg level. Wait for the iceberg to be exhausted; the move that follows is usually fast.
γ
Pulled Liquidity
Vanishing books in front of approaching price
Visual
A bright cluster goes dim or disappears as price closes in.
Meaning
Whoever placed those orders didn't actually want to defend the level. Their absence guarantees price moves through.
Action
A-grade signal in the direction of the move. If a candle trigger fires while liquidity is pulling ahead, take full size.
δ
Spoofing
Fake orders intended to mislead
Visual
Brief bright flashes that appear, sit briefly, and cancel without ever being tested. Often repeated at similar levels.
Meaning
Deliberately misleading display. Not real liquidity.
Action
Discount any apparent "level" being created by spoofing. Don't fade into it; don't trust it as support.
ε
CVD Divergence
Price and aggression disagreeing
Visual
Price making higher high, CVD making lower high (or vice versa for downside).
Meaning
The price move isn't being driven by real aggression in its direction. Fragile.
Action
Don't take candle triggers that align with the price direction during clear divergence. Wait for resolution.
ζ
Absorption
Price stalled despite heavy aggression
Visual
Tight price band, big bubbles arriving in one direction, heatmap stays bright at the level, CVD trends but price doesn't.
Meaning
Aggressive side throwing everything it has at a wall. Wall is winning.
Action
Skip candle triggers in the aggressive direction. The reversal is the trade — but for now, observe only.
η
Initiation
Aggression meets empty room
Visual
Aggressive push, resting liquidity ahead pulls or evaporates, price rolls through with continued aggression.
Meaning
The start of a real move. Bookmap's clearest "this is the trade" signal.
Action
If a candle trigger aligns with initiation, A-grade entry — take normal size. Most reliable scalp setup in the entire framework.
θ
Failed Initiation
A real start that dies
Visual
Initiation begins normally, then bubbles shrink, tape slows, heatmap rebuilds in the direction of the move.
Meaning
Aggression came in but was insufficient. Move dies. Reversal often follows.
Action
Skip candle triggers behind failed initiation — this is the pattern most associated with your 9.4% catastrophic losses.
Appendix B

Glossary

Absorption
Aggressive flow meeting passive resting orders that hold the level despite the aggression. Price doesn't move; the aggressive side exhausts.
Aggression
Market orders crossing the spread to execute immediately. The only thing that moves price.
Ask / Offer
The lowest price at which someone is willing to sell. Above current market.
Bid
The highest price at which someone is willing to buy. Below current market.
CVD (Cumulative Volume Delta)
Running sum of buy aggression minus sell aggression. Visualizes net flow over time.
Heatmap
Bookmap's visualization of resting limit orders over time. Brightness equals size; horizontal axis is time.
Iceberg
A large order displayed in small slices, refreshed as each slice is consumed. Hidden real liquidity.
Initiation
Aggressive flow that breaks through resting liquidity, starting a sustained move.
Limit Order
A passive order to buy or sell at a specific price, waiting for someone aggressive to take it.
Market Order
An aggressive order to buy or sell immediately at the best available price.
Pulled Liquidity
Resting orders that disappear from the book before being tested. Reveals lack of conviction by the placer.
Spoofing
Placing large orders without intent to execute, to manipulate other participants' decisions. Illegal but persistent.
Stacking
Multiple large orders building up at adjacent price levels, forming a defensive structure.
Volume Bubble
Bookmap visual of aggressive volume at a price/time. Size = contracts traded, color = direction (green buy / red sell).

Appendix E

30-Day Post-Course Integration Plan

Six weeks of observation built pattern recognition. Thirty days of disciplined application turns recognition into edge. The plan below is intentionally conservative — most of the failure modes in this kind of integration come from moving too fast.

Days 1-7 · Paper grading only
Observe and grade · do not change live behavior
Each session
Trade your candle system exactly as you do today. For every trigger you take or skip, silently grade the tape A/B/C/F before the outcome resolves. Log the grade. Match grade to outcome at end of day.
Days 8-14 · Skip-only filter
Begin acting on the filter — but only by skipping
Rule
If your live tape grade for a setup is F, skip the trade. Don't take any other actions yet. Don't change size on A or B trades. The first behavior to add is just refusing the worst.
Days 15-21 · Full sizing matrix
Apply size scaling per the grade table
Rule
A = full size. B = half. C = quarter or skip. F = skip. Trade this for one full week. Daily journal entries continue.
Days 22-30 · Review and refine
Audit the data and adjust the filter
Day 22
Sort journal by tape grade. Calculate win rate, average MAE, and expectancy in each bucket.
Day 23-29
Continue trading the framework. Note any cases where your gut overrode the framework and what happened.
Day 30
Final review. Decide whether the framework adds measurable value. If yes, formalize it permanently. If not, identify which check needs revision and run another 30-day cycle.
